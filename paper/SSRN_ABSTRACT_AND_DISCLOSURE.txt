Sequence scores offer a way to prioritize microproteins encoded by noncanonical open reading frames and propose edits that alter their accumulation. We reanalysed published measurements to test whether these scores remain useful across reporter contexts, independent peptide datasets and paired sequence edits. Among 1,072 development constructs, an annotation-based composition score correlated negatively with reporter accumulation (Spearman rho=-0.292), whereas models trained on accumulation reached 0.747 using composition and 0.780 with a richer terminal-aware representation. On the same 1,044 peptides across reporters, the within-GFP advantage of the richer representation was uncertain (Deltarho=0.021, conditional 95% interval -0.005 to 0.046). When applied to microprotein--mCherry, the GFP-trained richer model reached rho=0.560, compared with 0.663 for composition alone and 0.742 for a model trained on the target reporter. The paired composition-minus-richer contrast was 0.103 (conditional 95% interval 0.078 to 0.132). Tests on independent canonical peptides and 1,743 paired sequence changes showed that model comparisons also depended on input coverage, training exposure and whether the task was ranking peptides, selecting a subset or predicting edit effects. Strong published baselines reduced several apparent advantages. These retrospective benchmarks quantify predictive portability within the tested populations; they do not isolate causal effects of reporter components. Performance in one reporter does not justify sequence editing or use in another assay without testing. Native turnover and peptide-dependent function require separate experiments.
